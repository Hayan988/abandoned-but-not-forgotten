Why Does NASA Leave Spacecraft Behind?

Returning hardware from deep space or planetary surfaces requires exponential amounts of propellant (governed by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation). Instead, NASA retires or disposes of hardware for five primary scientific and engineering reasons:

1. Fuel & Power Exhaustion: Batteries freeze during two-week lunar nights, Martian dust coats solar panels, plutonium RTGs decay over decades, or attitude propellant runs dry.
2. Mass & Design Constraints: Crewed lunar landers and rovers were engineered for one-way delivery to maximize return fuel and sample payload for astronauts.
3. Planetary Protection: Outer-planet orbiters (like Cassini and Galileo) are intentionally vaporized in gas-giant atmospheres so Earth microbes cannot contaminate potentially habitable ocean moons like Enceladus or Europa.
4. Orbital Debris Mitigation: Satellites and lunar orbiters are deorbited into oceans or uninhabited lunar zones to prevent collisions with active missions or historic Apollo heritage sites.
5. Hardware & Software Anomalies: Extreme thermal cycles, metric-to-imperial navigation errors, or memory rollovers occasionally sever communications permanently.
